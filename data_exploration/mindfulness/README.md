# What mindfulness is

This note distinguishes mindfulness itself from the things one may be mindful of.
It uses the English translations and collection descriptions in this repository.

## Short definition

**Mindfulness is present-centered remembering: the sustained capacity to keep the
relevant object and purpose in mind without losing track of them.**

The body, feelings, mind, and principles are things on which mindfulness is
established. They are not, by themselves, definitions of mindfulness.

## The basic faculty: remembering

The collection description for SN 47 explains that the root sense of the Pali
*sati* is "memory." In meditation, this means not forgetting the meditation but
keeping its theme, such as the breath, in focus. It also distinguishes *sati*
from *sampajanna*, situational awareness or clear comprehension, which provides
broader awareness and understanding, especially during daily activities.[^1]

SN 48.10 supports this connection with memory. In its definition of the
mindfulness faculty, a mindful disciple is able to "remember and recall what was
said and done long ago," after which the discourse connects this faculty with
observing body, feelings, mind, and principles.[^2]

Thus "memory" here does not mean merely thinking about the past. It is the
faculty of retaining something so that it is not lost from the mind. Applied in
the present, mindfulness retains the meditation theme, relevant context, or
purpose.

## The practical function: not losing focus

SN 47.20 compares mindfulness of the body to carrying a bowl filled to the brim
with oil through a distracting crowd while someone with a drawn sword follows
behind. The carrier would not "lose focus on that bowl" or become negligently
distracted.[^3]

The simile highlights three features:

1. **Continuity:** the bowl must remain in mind throughout the task.
2. **Non-forgetfulness:** attractions in the surrounding crowd do not erase the
   carrier's purpose.
3. **Care:** mindfulness is not passive registration; it guards against a lapse.

Mindfulness therefore does not require the disappearance of all competing
sounds, sights, or thoughts. It means that they do not make the practitioner
lose the thread of what is being maintained.

## Mindfulness and awareness are related but distinct

SN 47.35 asks separately what it means to be "mindful" and what it means to be
"aware." Being mindful is described through sustained observation of body,
feelings, mind, and principles. Being aware is described as knowing feelings,
thoughts, and perceptions as they arise, remain, and pass away.[^4]

This gives a useful working distinction:

| Faculty | Function |
| --- | --- |
| Attention | Selects or turns toward something. |
| Mindfulness (*sati*) | Keeps the relevant object and purpose in mind without forgetting them. |
| Situational awareness (*sampajanna*) | Knows the wider situation and how experiences arise, remain, and cease. |
| Immersion or concentration (*samadhi*) | Unifies and stabilizes the mind. |
| Wisdom (*panna*) | Understands experience and its implications. |

The last three descriptions in this table are a concise interpretive summary,
not quotations or exhaustive canonical definitions.

## How broad should the topic be?

The suttas do not prescribe one fixed width. They move between narrowly
discriminating a feature, experiencing a whole field, and remaining aware during
a complete activity. The appropriate scope depends on what allows the theme to
remain clear without making the mind either scattered or cramped.

The image of a flashlight is useful with one qualification:

- **Attention is the beam:** it illuminates a narrower or broader area.
- **Mindfulness remembers the assignment:** it keeps the chosen theme and the
  purpose of looking in mind.
- **Situational awareness knows the context:** it recognizes what is happening
  in and around the illuminated area.

So mindfulness is not necessarily a tightly focused beam. One may be mindful
with narrow attention or with broad attention, provided the relevant theme has
not been forgotten.

### Four workable widths

| Width | Example | What remains "on topic" |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Point-like | The beginning or end of one breath | A specific feature of the chosen process |
| Object-wide | One complete in-breath and out-breath | The breathing process as a whole |
| Field-wide | The whole body while breathing | Bodily experience, with the breath included |
| Context-wide | Walking, eating, speaking, or the changing state of mind | The whole activity and its purpose |

This table is a practical interpretation, but the progression has textual
support. MN 10 first distinguishes heavy and light breathing, then expands to
experiencing the whole body. It proceeds to knowing the body's overall posture
and to situational awareness throughout ordinary activities.[^16] The same
discourse also moves between observing internally, externally, and both, and
observing arising and vanishing.[^17]

This means that being merely "on topic" can be sufficient. If the theme is
"the body while breathing," sensations in the chest, abdomen, posture, and
general bodily tone may all belong to the field. A sound can be present at the
edge without breaking mindfulness. But following the sound into a story and
forgetting the body would be a lapse.

### Narrow is not always stronger

A narrow beam can make a faint theme distinct and help gather a scattered mind.
But excessive narrowing can produce tension, exclude useful context, or turn
steadiness into forced fixation. A broad field can reveal relationships and
make practice more continuous in daily life. But excessive breadth can become
vague monitoring in which no theme is actually retained.

SN 47.10 recognizes both directed and undirected development. When the mind is
sluggish, uncomfortable, or scattered, the practitioner deliberately directs
it to an inspiring theme. Once the purpose is accomplished, that direction is
relaxed; the mind remains mindful without continually forcing the focus.[^18]
This suggests that deliberate narrowing can be a temporary tool rather than the
definition of mature mindfulness.

AN 3.102 gives a related balancing principle. Focusing only on immersion tends
toward laziness, only on exertion toward restlessness, and only on equanimity may
fail to establish immersion. The practitioner alternates among them so the mind
becomes workable rather than brittle.[^19] The discourse is about balancing
mental qualities, not beam width specifically, but it cautions against treating
one attentional mode as universally correct.

### A practical aperture test

Use the broadest field in which the theme remains unmistakable:

1. If you repeatedly forget the theme, narrow the field and make the object more
  definite.
2. If attention is steady but tense, cramped, or oblivious to the body and
  context, gently widen the field.
3. If broad awareness becomes foggy, identify one clear anchor within it without
  discarding the wider context.
4. Judge the width by continuity, clarity, and its effects: does it reduce
  grasping and distress, or increase strain, dullness, and distraction?

The resulting model is less like holding a flashlight permanently at its
narrowest setting and more like using an adjustable beam. Mindfulness keeps the
task from being forgotten while wisdom and situational awareness help adjust
the aperture.

## How does knowledge arise through mindfulness?

It is not simply "staring at the facts." Staring may keep an object present, but
knowledge requires recognizing relevant features, relationships, causes, and
changes. In the suttas, mindfulness makes sustained investigation possible; it
does not replace investigation or wisdom.

SN 46.3 gives an explicit sequence. A practitioner recollects and thinks about
the teaching, establishing mindfulness. While living mindfully, they
"investigate, explore, and inquire" into it with wisdom. This arouses energy,
followed by rapture, tranquility, immersion, and equanimity.[^20] Mindfulness is
the first awakening factor in the sequence; investigation of principles is the
second.

### What mindfulness contributes

Mindfulness contributes at least three things:

1. **Continuity of evidence:** the experience remains available long enough to
  be examined instead of being immediately forgotten or replaced by a story.
2. **Recognition:** the practitioner remembers the relevant categories and
  questions, such as "Is desire present? What preceded it? What makes it fade?"
3. **Reduced interference:** as reactivity settles and the mind becomes
  tranquil and immersed, observation is less distorted by grasping, aversion,
  restlessness, and dullness.

The first two are closely tied to mindfulness. The third belongs to the wider
training represented by the awakening factors. The distinction matters:
mindfulness supplies stable, remembered contact with the evidence, while
investigation and wisdom discern what the evidence shows.

### From a fact to a pattern

MN 10 does not instruct someone merely to stare at "desire." It says to
understand:

- whether desire is present or absent;
- how it arises;
- how it is given up; and
- how it can cease to arise in the future.[^21]

It applies the same structure to ill will, dullness, restlessness, and doubt,
and also examines the origin and disappearance of the aggregates and the
fetters that arise through sense contact.[^21]

For example, mindfulness may keep anger clearly present without suppressing or
feeding it. Investigation can then notice a sequence:

> painful feeling -> rehearsing an interpretation -> bodily tightening -> urge
> to retaliate

It may also notice that withholding the rehearsal weakens the tightening and
urge. The knowledge is not simply "anger exists." It is conditional knowledge:
"given these conditions, anger grows; when this condition is removed, it
fades." The arrow sequence here is a modern illustrative example, not a
quotation from MN 10.

### Seeing change repeatedly

The mindfulness instructions repeatedly turn observation toward origination
and vanishing. MN 10 establishes mindfulness only "to the extent necessary for
knowledge and mindfulness," without grasping at what is observed.[^22]
Mindfulness of breathing likewise develops from knowing the breath and mental
state to observing impermanence, fading away, cessation, and letting go. It then
moves explicitly from lucid mindfulness to investigation with wisdom.[^23]

Repeated observation matters because a single instance can be misleading. When
the same pattern is seen across many instances, assumptions such as "this state
is permanent," "this appeared without conditions," or "I must obey this urge"
become harder to maintain. Knowledge arises through accumulated discrimination,
not through duration of staring alone.

### Observation is active, but not discursive all the time

"Investigation" need not mean continually narrating or theorizing. Early in
practice, a deliberate question can orient attention. Once the relevant pattern
is clear, quieter observation may reveal it directly. Too much commentary can
replace the experience with thoughts about it; too little discrimination can
leave awareness vague.

A practical cycle is:

1. **Remember:** retain the chosen domain and purpose.
2. **Recognize:** know what quality is present.
3. **Investigate:** notice its conditions, changes, effects, and cessation.
4. **Verify:** see whether the pattern recurs rather than accepting one
  impression.
5. **Release:** when grasping is seen as conditional and stressful, stop feeding
  it.

Thus mindfulness is epistemically useful because it prevents the evidence and
the question from being lost. Knowledge arises when that continuity is joined
with appropriate investigation, a sufficiently clear and settled mind, and
wisdom about conditionality.

## How mindfulness is increased

The suttas present mindfulness as a faculty strengthened by cultivation and
repetition. In practical terms, each time the intended theme is forgotten and
then re-established, the act of re-establishing it is part of the training. The
aim is not to prevent every distraction from appearing, but to recognize a
lapse and resume without letting the thread remain lost.

### 1. Prepare supportive conditions

SN 47.3 calls purified ethics and correct view the "starting point" and says to
develop the four kinds of mindfulness meditation grounded on them.[^5] Conduct
matters because remorse, conflict, and agitation make continuity of mind harder.
For formal practice, SN 54.1 also recommends a secluded setting, an upright
posture, and bringing mindfulness to the present.[^6]

### 2. Choose a clear theme and repeatedly remember it

A simple exercise based on SN 54.1 is:

1. Sit upright somewhere reasonably quiet.
2. Bring the breathing to mind without forcing it.
3. Know a long or heavy breath as long or heavy, and a short or light breath as
  short or light.
4. When the breath has been forgotten, recognize that and re-establish it as the
  theme.
5. Continue cultivating awareness of the whole body and calming the bodily
  process.[^6]

The crucial repetition is **remember, notice the lapse, return**. The return is
not a failure of practice; it is the renewal of mindfulness.

Breathing is only one possible theme. The four establishments described in
SN 47.3 are body, feelings, mind, and principles, contemplated internally,
externally, and both internally and externally.[^5]

### 3. Learn what supports steadiness

SN 47.8 compares a skillful meditator to a cook who notices which dishes please
the ruler. The practitioner "takes the mind's hint": they notice when a way of
practicing leads to immersion and the weakening of corruptions, and when it does
not.[^7] Increasing mindfulness therefore includes learning from conditions,
not just applying force. Posture, time, meditation theme, energy, and the
presence of hindrances can all be observed and adjusted.

SN 47.10 gives a specific adjustment for sluggishness, bodily discomfort, or an
externally scattered mind: temporarily direct the mind to an inspiring
meditation theme until joy, tranquility, and collectedness arise, then relax
that deliberate direction.[^8]

### 4. Carry it into ordinary activities

MN 10 extends practice beyond sitting. One knows when one is walking, standing,
sitting, or lying down, and exercises situational awareness while looking,
moving, eating, speaking, and remaining silent.[^9] A manageable application is
to choose one recurring activity, such as standing up or opening a door, and
use it as a cue to remember the body, current state of mind, and present
intention.

The general pattern is therefore:

> Establish a suitable theme, retain it, recognize when it has been lost,
> re-establish it, and learn which conditions make that continuity stronger.

## Cases of weak or lost mindfulness

The suttas rarely treat weak mindfulness as an isolated trait. It usually
appears together with negligence, lack of situational awareness, a scattered
mind, unguarded senses, or indulgence. The consequences also vary with whether
the condition is persistent or only a temporary lapse.

### A distracted monk becomes vulnerable to unskillful thoughts

In AN 3.128 the Buddha sees a disgruntled monk seeking pleasure externally. The
monk is unmindful, unaware, unimmersed, mentally scattered, and unrestrained in
his senses. The Buddha explains that desire, ill will, and unskillful thoughts
will "plague" such a person. Their result is distress and being far from
extinguishment. The warning works: the monk is struck with a sense of urgency.[^10]

This is the clearest psychological consequence: without mindfulness and
restraint, an attractive or disagreeable experience more easily develops into a
train of desire, aversion, and distress.

### A group of monks declines in conduct

In SN 9.13 several monks are described as unmindful, scattered, and with
undisciplined faculties. Their weakness is visible in their behavior: they are
arrogant, loose-tongued, demanding, and given to eating and lying down. A forest
deity contrasts them with diligent practitioners and admonishes them. They too
respond with a sense of urgency.[^11]

Ud 4.2 describes a similar group. The Buddha says that, unguarded and overcome
by dullness and drowsiness, they fall under Mara's sway.[^12] In these passages,
weak mindfulness is part of a broader loss of self-regulation rather than a
mere inability to concentrate.

### Prolonged distraction ends a fortunate state

DN 1 gives a cosmological example of gods called "depraved by play." They spend
so long laughing, playing, and making merry that they lose mindfulness and pass
away from that divine realm.[^13] Within the story, the consequence of prolonged
heedless absorption in entertainment is the loss of a fortunate but impermanent
state. This is an unusual cosmological case, not a claim that ordinary enjoyment
itself causes immediate death.

### A temporary lapse need not become a collapse

SN 35.244 explicitly allows that even a restrained practitioner may lose
mindfulness occasionally. Unskillful memories and thoughts then arise, but when
mindfulness returns the practitioner quickly abandons them, like drops of water
evaporating on a heated iron cauldron.[^14] The danger is therefore not that a
single distraction has appeared, but that it is sustained, followed, or allowed
to overwhelm the mind.

SN 55.21 makes the qualification stronger. Mahānāma worries that he might die
after losing recollection of the Buddha, teaching, and Saṅgha amid busy traffic.
The Buddha reassures him that a momentary lapse does not cancel a mind long
shaped by faith, ethics, learning, generosity, and wisdom.[^15]

Taken together, these cases suggest a progression:

1. Mindfulness lapses and an unskillful thought gains an opening.
2. If the lapse continues, desire, aversion, dullness, or scattered thinking can
  take over.
3. Repeated negligence can show up as deteriorating conduct and stalled
  development.
4. Promptly restored mindfulness interrupts the process, while deeply cultivated
  qualities provide resilience even when a lapse occurs.

## Ordinary examples

- While reading, attention lands on the page; mindfulness remembers that one is
  reading when a train of thought begins to carry the mind away.
- When anger is present, mindfulness retains the recognition "anger is present"
  and the intention not to act blindly from it.
- During breath meditation, mindfulness keeps the breath as the meditation
  theme. Situational awareness recognizes changes in the experience and in the
  state of mind.

A lapse of mindfulness is therefore not simply the appearance of a distraction.
It is losing the relevant object, context, or purpose from mind.
