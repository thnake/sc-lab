# Recognizing the abandonment of the five hindrances

## Research question

DN 2 describes the transition from abandoning the five hindrances to gladness,
rapture, tranquility, happiness, and immersion:

> *Tassime pañca nīvaraṇe pahīne attani samanupassato pāmojjaṁ jāyati,
> pamuditassa pīti jāyati, pītimanassa kāyo passambhati, passaddhakāyo sukhaṁ
> vedeti, sukhino cittaṁ samādhiyati.*[^1]

The significant expression is:

> *pañca nīvaraṇe pahīne attani samanupassato pāmojjaṁ jāyati*

This report asks whether the passage presents gladness as the direct result of
the hindrances merely being absent, or as arising when the practitioner
recognizes their abandonment.

## Conclusion

In this formula, recognition is an explicit link in the sequence. The grammar
says that gladness arises **for one who sees the five hindrances as abandoned in
themselves**. Exact parallels in DN 9, DN 10, and DN 13 preserve the same link.
The surrounding similes, MN 40, and AN 5.157 reinforce the pattern that
recognizing a beneficial change or quality in oneself gives rise to gladness.

This supports the following sequence:

> abandonment of the hindrances -> recognition of their abandonment ->
> gladness -> rapture -> bodily tranquility -> happiness -> immersion

The evidence does not establish that unrecognized relief can never produce any
pleasant feeling. Its narrower claim is that the canonical sequence under
consideration explicitly makes reflective recognition the condition associated
with *pāmojja*.

## The grammar of the DN 2 formula

The relevant words are:

| Pali | Function | Sense in context |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *tassa* | dative singular | for him; for that practitioner |
| *ime pañca nīvaraṇe* | accusative plural | these five hindrances |
| *pahīne* | accusative plural | abandoned, given up |
| *attani* | locative singular | in oneself |
| *samanupassato* | genitive/dative singular present participle | for one seeing, observing, or recognizing |
| *pāmojjaṁ jāyati* | subject and verb | gladness arises |

The participle *samanupassato* matters. The sentence does not simply say
"when the five hindrances are abandoned, gladness arises." It identifies the
person for whom gladness arises as one who sees or recognizes them as abandoned
in themselves.

The preceding DN 2 segment states the act of recognition separately:

> *bhikkhu ime pañca nīvaraṇe pahīne attani samanupassati.*
>
> A mendicant sees these five hindrances as abandoned in themselves.[^2]

The next segment converts *samanupassati*, "they see," into the participial
*samanupassato*, "for one seeing this," and continues with the sequence leading
to immersion.

The passage does not use the noun *ñāṇa*, "knowledge." It is therefore safest
to speak of seeing, recognizing, observing, or discerning the changed condition
rather than introducing a technical knowledge not named in the sentence.

## Exact parallels

The complete formula occurs in four discourses:

| Discourse | Segment | Context following the formula |
| --- | --- | --- |
| DN 2, *Sāmaññaphala Sutta* | 74.4 | the four absorptions |
| DN 9, *Poṭṭhapāda Sutta* | 9.1 | the arising and cessation of trained perceptions |
| DN 10, *Subha Sutta* | 2.12.4 | the four absorptions |
| DN 13, *Tevijja Sutta* | 40-75.4 | the four divine abidings |

DN 9 and DN 13 abbreviate the preceding training, but retain the whole sentence
from seeing abandoned hindrances through immersion.[^3] DN 10 supplies the full
context and is particularly useful because it states in successive segments:

1. The practitioner sees the unabandoned hindrances in themselves as debt,
   sickness, imprisonment, slavery, and a dangerous journey.
2. The practitioner sees the abandoned hindrances in themselves as freedom
   from debt, health, release, emancipation, and safety.
3. For the practitioner seeing the hindrances abandoned in themselves,
   gladness arises.[^4]

The repetition makes it difficult to treat *samanupassato* as incidental. The
text first names the appraisal, then makes that appraisal part of the transition
to gladness.

## The five similes: conscious comparison and relief

DN 2, DN 10, and MN 39 explain the hindrances using five similes:

| Hindrance as burden | Abandonment as release |
| --- | --- |
| debt | freedom from debt |
| illness | health |
| imprisonment | release from prison |
| slavery | emancipation |
| a dangerous desert journey | arrival at safety |

The people in the similes do not merely undergo a changed condition. They think
about the contrast between their former and present states. For example, the
formerly enslaved person reflects, in effect, "Previously I was a slave ... but
now I am free, independent, and able to go where I wish." The formula then
says:

> *So tatonidānaṁ labhetha pāmojjaṁ, adhigaccheyya somanassaṁ.*
>
> Because of that, they would gain gladness and obtain happiness.[^5]

The compound *tatonidānaṁ*, "because of that" or "with that as its source,"
follows the person's conscious comparison. The similes therefore model not
only release but known release: a person appreciates that a former burden is no
longer present.

MN 39 preserves all five similes and concludes:

> *ime pañca nīvaraṇe appahīne attani samanupassati* ...
>
> *ime pañca nīvaraṇe pahīne attani samanupassati.*[^6]

That is, the practitioner sees the five hindrances first as unabandoned and then
as abandoned in themselves. MN 39 proceeds directly to first absorption rather
than repeating the gladness formula, but it independently confirms the same
reflective appraisal.

## MN 40: seeing oneself purified

MN 40 applies nearly the same syntax to purification from a wider group of
unskillful qualities, including covetousness, ill will, anger, hostility,
jealousy, stinginess, deceit, corrupt wishes, and wrong view:

> *So sabbehi imehi pāpakehi akusalehi dhammehi visuddhamattānaṁ
> samanupassati.*
>
> They see themselves purified from all these bad, unskillful qualities.

It immediately continues:

> *Tassa ... visuddhamattānaṁ samanupassato pāmojjaṁ jāyati ...*
>
> For one seeing themselves purified ... gladness arises.[^7]

This is a strong structural parallel. Once again, purification is followed by
seeing oneself as purified, and the participle "for one seeing" introduces the
arising of gladness.

## AN 5.157: recognizing qualities as a source of gladness

AN 5.157 provides a useful general statement about self-recognition and
*pītipāmojja*, "rapture and gladness." It applies the same pattern to five
accomplishments: faith, ethical conduct, learning, generosity, and wisdom.
For ethical conduct, for example, it says:

> *Tañhi so sīlasampadaṁ attani samanupassati, labhati ca tatonidānaṁ
> pītipāmojjaṁ.*
>
> They see that accomplishment in ethical conduct in themselves, and because
> of that obtain rapture and gladness.[^8]

The discourse repeats the same syntax for all five qualities:

> quality in oneself + *samanupassati* -> *tatonidānaṁ pītipāmojjaṁ*

This is especially relevant because both key expressions from the hindrance
passage recur: *attani samanupassati*, seeing in oneself, and *pāmojja*,
gladness. It demonstrates a broader canonical pattern in which appreciating a
wholesome condition in oneself is a source of gladness.

The negative half of AN 5.157 says that an unfaithful, unethical, unlearned,
stingy, or unwise person does not see the corresponding accomplishment in
themselves and therefore does not obtain the rapture and gladness arising from
it.[^9] This contrast concerns people who lack the named accomplishment; it
does not independently prove that an existing but unnoticed quality produces
no gladness. The positive half nevertheless makes recognition an explicit part
of the stated causal pattern.

## MN 10 and DN 22: knowing presence, absence, and abandonment

The Satipaṭṭhāna instructions in MN 10 and DN 22 require the practitioner to
understand each hindrance in several respects. Taking sensual desire as the
example, the practitioner understands:

1. when sensual desire is present internally;
2. when it is absent internally;
3. how unarisen sensual desire arises;
4. how arisen sensual desire is abandoned; and
5. how an abandoned sensual desire does not arise again in the future.[^10]

The Pali repeatedly uses *pajānāti*, "understands" or "clearly knows." These
passages do not connect that understanding directly to the DN 2 gladness
sequence. They do show, however, that knowing a hindrance's presence, absence,
abandonment, and future non-arising is itself an explicit part of practice.
Recognition of abandonment is therefore not foreign to the treatment of the
hindrances elsewhere in the Nikāyas.

## Can bodily calming provide another entry into the sequence?

The latter part of the sequence supports a direct progression from tranquility
through happiness to immersion, but the Pali terms must be distinguished:

> *pīti* -> *passaddhi* -> *sukha* -> *samādhi*

Here *pīti* is rapture, *passaddhi* is tranquility, and *sukha* is happiness,
pleasure, or ease. Bodily tranquility therefore does not generate rapture in
the DN 2 formula. Rapture precedes tranquility; a tranquil body experiences
*sukha*, and the mind of one who is happy becomes immersed.

AN 10.2 describes both transitions as natural consequences that do not have to
be produced by an additional act of will:

> *Dhammatā esā ... yaṁ passaddhakāyo sukhaṁ vediyati.*
>
> It is natural that one whose body is tranquil experiences happiness.

> *Dhammatā esā ... yaṁ sukhino cittaṁ samādhiyati.*
>
> It is natural that the mind of one who is happy becomes immersed.[^11]

MN 118 and SN 46.3 identify the same transition with the development of the
tranquility and immersion awakening factors. They first say that both body and
mind become tranquil--*kāyopi passambhati, cittampi passambhati*--and then that
the happy mind of one whose body is tranquil becomes immersed.[^12] This warns
against interpreting *passaddhakāya* as physical immobility alone. The relevant
condition is an integrated psychophysical settling, although it includes a
bodily dimension.

There is nevertheless a canonical basis for deliberately entering the process
from that bodily dimension. Mindfulness of breathing includes the training:

> *passambhayaṁ kāyasaṅkhāraṁ assasissāmi ... passasissāmi*
>
> I will breathe in ... and breathe out calming the bodily process.[^13]

MN 44 identifies the bodily process in this context as inhalation and
exhalation, since breathing is bodily and bound up with the body.[^14] Calming
breathing and bodily activation can therefore contribute to ease and mental
stability. The standard meditation instructions also establish an upright,
settled posture before mindfulness is applied.[^15]

The texts do not, however, state the universal sequence:

> physical stillness alone -> abandonment of all five hindrances

SN 46.51 assigns different ways of starving the five hindrances. Attention to
unattractiveness counters sensual desire; loving-kindness counters ill will;
initiative and exertion counter dullness; mental settling counters restlessness
and remorse; and investigation of skillful and unskillful qualities counters
doubt.[^16] Bodily calming is most directly relevant to agitation. Without
alertness and investigation, mere immobility could coexist with dullness,
desire, ill will, or doubt.

A cautious practical model is therefore a reinforcing loop:

> calming posture and breath -> reduced bodily activation -> greater ease ->
> increasing mental stability -> weakening of hindrances -> recognition of
> their absence -> gladness -> rapture -> deeper tranquility -> happiness ->
> stronger immersion

Not every link in this expanded model occurs as one continuous canonical
formula. It synthesizes the breath instructions, the hindrance remedies, and
the repeated tranquility-to-immersion sequence. Once tranquility and *sukha*
are already present, there is no need for the original sequence to "restart"
at gladness: AN 10.2, MN 118, and SN 46.3 say that *sukha* itself is an immediate
support for samādhi.

## Interpretation

The collected evidence supports three levels of claim.

### Strong textual claim

In DN 2, DN 9, DN 10, and DN 13, the grammatical subject experiencing the
arising of gladness is qualified as one who sees the five hindrances abandoned
in themselves. Recognition belongs to the wording of the sequence.

### Strong contextual claim

The five similes depict agents who compare their former bondage with their
present freedom and gain gladness "because of that." MN 40 and AN 5.157 use the
same language of seeing a purified or wholesome condition in oneself as the
basis for gladness.

### Claim the evidence does not establish

These passages do not prove that the mere weakening or temporary absence of a
hindrance can have no pleasant psychological effect unless one conceptually
labels it. Nor do they specify that the recognition must be discursive or
verbal. *Samanupassati* can refer to a direct, sustained seeing or discernment,
not necessarily an internal sentence such as "my hindrances are gone."

The most defensible conclusion is therefore:

> Abandonment supplies the freedom, but in this canonical sequence it is the
> practitioner's recognition of that freedom which immediately precedes and
> conditions gladness.

A further terminological caution is needed. *Pahīna* means abandoned or given
up, not merely reduced. In practical experience the text may be applied to the
settling of hindrances sufficient for meditation, but "reduction" is a weaker
claim than the Pali wording itself.
